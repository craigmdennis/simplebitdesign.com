## Customers were leaving over quality no one could see coming

Customers cancelled after a bad stretch of calls, and by the time anyone noticed, the refund request had already arrived. The AI was handling real conversations at scale, but nobody inside the company had a shared, honest read on whether a given customer's experience was getting better or worse. Quality lived in scattered anecdotes and the occasional angry ticket.

## It looked like a support problem. It was a measurement problem.

The instinct was to staff up support and apologise faster. That treats the symptom. The real issue was that we could not see a quality dip while it was happening, so every recovery started too late. If we could make quality visible, a dip could become something we caught and fixed instead of something a customer caught and cancelled over.

[VERIFY with Craig: the early attempts. Likely candidates from the work: per-call review, manual spot checks, a dashboard that nobody trusted.] The honest version is that the first cut measured what was easy to count rather than what actually predicted churn, so the team didn't act on it.

## A single score the whole company could act on

I designed one quality score, built from analysis of real call quality, that ops, product, and leadership could all read the same way. It turned "I think things feel worse" into a number you could watch move, attach to a customer, and act on before the cancellation.

The design work was deciding what the score should reward, how to surface it so a non-analyst could act on it, and where it belonged in the day-to-day so it changed behaviour rather than sitting in a report.
